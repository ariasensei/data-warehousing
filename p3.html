Introduction

Imagine that you own a coffee shop with three branches. Every day, hundreds of customers buy coffee, pastries, and other drinks. After one year, you have over 100,000 sales records.

Your manager now asks questions like:

  • Which drink sells the most?
  • Which branch earns the highest revenue?
  • Which month has the highest sales?
  • Which customers visit the most often?

If all of your data is stored in many separate tables, answering these questions could take a long time. A Data Warehouse solves this problem by organizing data in a way that makes reporting and analysis much faster.

CORE IDEA //

This method of organizing data is called Dimensional Modelling.

What is Dimensional Modelling?

Think of a library. Imagine all the books are thrown into one giant pile. Finding one book would be difficult. Now imagine the books are organized by:

Subject
Author
Year
Genre

Finding a book becomes much easier. A Data Warehouse works the same way. Instead of organizing books, it organizes business data so computers can answer questions quickly.

Definition

Dimensional Modelling is a technique used in Data Warehousing to organize data for reporting and decision-making. Instead of designing a database mainly for storing data, we design it so that users can analyze information quickly and easily.

Why Do We Need It?

Businesses collect a huge amount of data every day. Examples include sales transactions, student enrollments, hospital records, bank transactions, and online shopping orders.

Managers need answers quickly. They may ask, “What was our total sales last month?” or “Which product is our best seller?”

Without dimensional modelling, these reports would require complicated database queries and could take much longer to generate. Dimensional modelling helps organize data so reports are faster and easier to create.

Two Main Parts of Dimensional Modelling

Everything in dimensional modelling is built around two kinds of tables: Fact Tables and Dimension Tables. Think of them as answering different kinds of questions.

Fact Tables

“What happened?”

A Fact Table stores measurable information. These are usually numbers that can be calculated.

  • Sales Amount
  • Quantity Sold
  • Profit
  • Discount
  • Number of Students Enrolled

Fact tables answer: How many? How much? How often?

Dimension Tables

“Tell me more about it.”

Dimension tables contain descriptive information. Instead of numbers, they describe the facts.

  • Who bought the product?
  • What product was purchased?
  • When was it purchased?
  • Where did the sale happen?

Dimensions provide business context for every fact.

Example: Sales Fact Table

Sale ID Product Customer Date Quantity Sales
1001 Laptop John July 1 2 ₱60,000
1002 Mouse Maria July 1 5 ₱7,500

Notice that the important values are the numbers: Quantity and Sales. These are the facts.

Facts answer

“How much?”

Dimensions answer

Who? What? Where? When? Why?

Star Schema

The Star Schema is the most common design used in Data Warehouses. It is called a Star Schema because the fact table is placed in the center, while the dimension tables surround it like the points of a star.

The Sales table contains the numbers, while the surrounding tables describe those numbers.

Easy to understand
Easy to design
Fast to query
Ideal for reports

Most Business Intelligence tools such as Power BI and Tableau work very well with Star Schemas.

Snowflake Schema

A Snowflake Schema is similar to a Star Schema, but the dimension tables are divided into smaller related tables.

SALES
PRODUCT
DATE
CATEGORY

For example, instead of storing Product and Category together, we separate Category into another table. This reduces duplicate information.

The trade-off is that the database becomes more organized, but queries become more complicated because there are more tables to join.

Star Schema Snowflake Schema
Simple structure More detailed structure
Faster queries Slightly slower queries
Easy to understand More complex
Uses more storage Uses less storage

For most reporting systems, organizations prefer the Star Schema because it is simpler and faster.

Grain

One of the most important decisions in dimensional modelling is choosing the grain. The grain tells us exactly what one row in the fact table represents.

One row can mean:

  • One sales transaction
  • One student enrollment
  • One hospital admission
  • One product sold per day

Think of the grain as answering this question: “What does one record in this table represent?”

If the grain is not clearly defined, reports may produce incorrect results.

Surrogate Keys

Many operational systems already have IDs. For example, a product may already have Product ID = P100. In Data Warehousing, we usually create another ID called a Surrogate Key.

Operational system

Product ID = P100

Warehouse key

ProductKey = 15

Surrogate keys make joins faster, never change, and help preserve historical records.

Advanced Concepts

Once we understand the basics, we can solve more complex business problems.

Slowly Changing Dimensions (SCD)

Managing information that changes over time

People and businesses change over time. Imagine a customer named Maria. In 2024 she lives in Manila. In 2026 she moves to Cebu. Should the warehouse replace her old address? That depends on the business requirement.

TYPE 1

Replace the old value

Maria – Manila becomes Maria – Cebu. The old address is lost.

TYPE 2

Keep both records

Maria – Manila and Maria – Cebu both remain. This preserves historical information.

TYPE 3

Keep one previous value

Current City: Cebu. Previous City: Manila. Useful when only recent history matters.

Type 2 is the most common approach because businesses often need historical information.

Different Types of Fact Tables

Transaction Fact Table

Stores one record for every transaction. Example: every purchase made in Shopee.

Periodic Snapshot

Stores information at regular intervals, such as daily inventory, monthly sales, or weekly attendance.

Accumulating Snapshot

Tracks a process from beginning to end, such as an online order: ordered, packed, shipped, and delivered.

Junk Dimension

Several small pieces of information can be grouped into one dimension. Examples include Gift Wrap, Payment Method, and Promo Used. This makes the database simpler.

Degenerate Dimension

Useful values that do not need their own table, such as Invoice Number, Receipt Number, or Order Number. These stay directly inside the Fact Table.

Role-Playing Dimension

The same dimension is used in different ways. One Date Dimension can represent Order Date, Shipping Date, and Delivery Date.

Conformed Dimensions

Large companies may share one Customer Dimension across Sales, Inventory, and Marketing fact tables to keep reports consistent.

Dimensional modelling is the foundation of Data Warehousing because it organizes data for fast reporting and business analysis. Every design starts with identifying the facts — measurable values — and dimensions — descriptive information. The Star Schema is the most widely used because it is simple and efficient, while the Snowflake Schema provides greater normalization at the cost of more complex queries.

Advanced concepts such as Slowly Changing Dimensions, different fact table types, surrogate keys, and conformed dimensions help organizations manage historical data and build scalable Business Intelligence systems. Understanding these concepts prepares students to design data warehouses that support real-world decision-making in retail, banking, healthcare, education, and e-commerce.
